The lighthouse at Marrow Bay had been without a keeper for eleven years when the grey cat arrived. Nobody saw her come. One morning in March there was simply a small shape on the gallery rail, washing a paw as though she had paid the rent.

The villagers noticed her first as a matter of arithmetic. Old Mr. Pell, who walked the sea wall every dawn, counted the gulls on the tower and found one fewer than usual. Then he found the cat. By April the whole village had an opinion about her, and by May the opinions had turned to affection.

The Lamp

It was the lamp that changed things. The light had been automated long ago and no one went up the stairs any more. But the cat did. Every evening, at the hour the beam swung on, she sat in the window of the lantern room with the light turning behind her, so that from the harbour she seemed to be a tiny black shape riding a wheel of gold.

Children began to wait on the quay to see her. Fishermen, who are not sentimental people, started to say that the night felt shorter with her up there. Someone left a saucer at the foot of the tower. Someone else left a blanket. Within a month the door was repaired, because it seemed rude to make her climb in through the broken window.

She never did tell anyone where she came from. In autumn the storms arrived, as storms do, and the villagers found themselves glancing up at the tower between gusts, checking that the small shape was still there. She always was.

Marrow Bay has a keeper again now, in a way. Nobody pays her, and she would not accept the money if they tried. But the stairs are swept, the lamp is polished, and every evening the village looks up.
